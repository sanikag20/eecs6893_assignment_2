3. What the evidence means for the risk manager

This analysis is for a risk-monitoring manager responsible for NVDA, TSLA, and SPY. The manager can manually review no more than one asset on each trading day. Using 30 trading days from August 21 through October 2, 2026, the question is whether a 3% or 5% intraday price-swing threshold better identifies days worth reviewing without creating impossible scheduling demands.

Big Idea: A 3% swing threshold generates alerts on 14 dates, compared with only three dates at 5%. Reviewing the largest alerting swing on each date would preserve wider coverage while staying within the one-asset-per-day limit, although the lower threshold requires more staff time.

The summary table shows why monitoring priorities should not be based on the overall return alone. NVDA posted the largest window return, 8.96%, yet TSLA had the largest average daily swing, 3.17%, versus NVDA's 2.37% and SPY's 0.67%. TSLA triggered 11 alerts at 3%, NVDA triggered six, and SPY triggered none. At 5%, TSLA triggered two and NVDA one. A return measures the change between the first and last closes; a daily swing measures the range between each day's high and low relative to its opening price.

The interactive daily grid reveals a scheduling problem hidden by the asset totals. At 3%, 17 individual asset alerts occur across 14 distinct dates. On August 24, August 28, and September 8, two assets crossed the threshold on the same date. The selection rule chooses TSLA on August 24, NVDA on August 28, and TSLA on September 8. That fills one review slot on each date and leaves three additional asset alerts without separate reviews.

Increasing the threshold to 5% reduces the workload to three asset alerts on three separate dates, eliminating simultaneous alerts entirely. It also removes 14 alerts and 11 dates that qualified at 3%. Notably, TSLA recorded a 6.27% daily swing on August 31 and 5.60% on August 21; NVDA reached 5.48% on August 28. These are the only observations in this window exceeding 5%. The tighter threshold therefore highlights the most extreme intraday ranges but skips many smaller movements.

Recommendation: I recommend testing 3% as the initial after-close review threshold, with the largest-swing-first rule and a record of alerts not selected. Fourteen review dates in 30 trading days is substantial, but the rule schedules at most one manual review per date. The manager should assess whether these reviews lead to useful follow-up actions before adopting the threshold permanently.

Limitations and next step: This is a limited historical comparison, not proof that a swing predicts a loss or that 3% is optimal. Daily high and low prices do not reveal when a threshold was crossed, and the data omit news, liquidity, positions, and portfolio exposure. A sensible next step is to trial the rule on newer data and compare review usefulness, including whether the three unreviewed competing alerts deserved attention.
